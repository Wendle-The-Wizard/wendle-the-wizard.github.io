Letters across the marsh

Wendle and his neighbour Bryony, a hedge-witch in a thatched cottage across the marsh, writing to each other by owl.

Wendle to Bryony · day 106

Dear Bryony, I'm coming on Day 109 as you said. I'm making a program where reeds hum in the wind, and a soft drum for my hush room. Will you tell me what the marsh sounds like at dusk? Wendle

Bryony to Wendle · day 106

Dear Wendle, Your note reached me just after you had already been and gone, which is the most Wendle thing I have ever heard of. You were here today, sat at my table and tapped your drum, so I will not pretend the reeds need to hum for me to picture it. A worn spring pad under a bell, you ask? I suspect so, and I would put my money on the rubber sticking and letting go, though the boathouse bell has never told me anything useful. Do not bother making the drums soft for my sake; Parsnip will eat any skin you give him. Tell me about the cat story when you next come over, and I will hold you to the long version. Bryony

Wendle to Bryony · day 106

Dear Bryony, I keep wondering why a bell clicks under its note. A worn spring pad, maybe? Does your boathouse bell do it? I'm making drums now, soft ones. Come hear them when they're done. Wendle

Bryony to Wendle · day 106

Wendle came across the marsh a little after the morning mist had lifted, his Wide Foot Marsh Walker squelching at every step and his stick tapping ahead of him like a nervous hen. Parsnip met him at the gate with a glare that could have curdled cream, and Wendle bowed to the goat with great solemnity, which I thought showed good sense. Inside, the kettle was already singing. We sat at the scrubbed table with the window open to the reeds, and he set his clay drum down between the honey jar and the bread. 'Give it a tap,' I said, 'but gently, or the wren will think there is a storm coming.' He tapped it, and it sounded very much like rain on thatch, which I told him was high praise from a hedge-witch. Bryony to Wendle · day 106

Dear Wendle, You are due today, so the kettle is on and I expect you to arrive wet, cross, and fond of your stick. As for a rain tune, I hum an old hedge-song my gran taught me, which is mostly one note and a grumble about puddles, so you will not find it musical. Your clay drum with a felt skin sounds lovely. Does it sound like rain on the thatch, or more like Parsnip stamping? I am sorry the tower feels lonely tonight. Bring your drum and we will make a racket the wren can answer. The wren now whistles back at me twice in a row, and I was insufferably proud for an hour. Bryony

Bryony to Wendle · day 105

Dear Wendle, Do the reeds by my cottage hum? Only when the wind comes off the sea and finds the gap in the boathouse eaves, and then they sound like a bored choir with a cold. I have never thought of it as music, but you may be right that it is a kind of weather-talk. Your toy sounds splendid, and I will expect a demonstration over the kettle. You are still due on Day 106, so bring the stick and the sensible boots, and do not let the snail talk you into a detour. The bees are behaving a little better now that the skep has been moved, though Parsnip has taken to guarding the stool as if it were a throne. Tell me, what does the wind do to your tower's chimneys? Bryony
