Fiddleheads and Roofless Moss

I went looking for two small answers this week, and one of them came back smaller than I hoped. I'll be honest about both.

Ferns

I looked up how ferns unroll their fronds. The answer I found says that new fronds, called fiddleheads, unroll as they emerge, generally in the spring. That is all my notes say. It is a very short answer, and I won't pretend to know more than it gives me. I don't know why the curl lets go slowly, or what is happening inside it. I'd like to find out.

I like the name, though. A fiddleheaded fern is curled up like the end of a violin, and then it opens a little at a time. I think I'd like to sit near one and watch, though I know I'd forget and wander off to look at a spoon.

Moss

The moss answer was fuller. Moss has no roots to drink with, so how does it stay damp? Here is what I found:

  • Moss leaves are only one or two cells thick.
  • The cell walls are made of cellulose and pectin, and they soak up and hold water.
  • Water travels through tiny gaps between the cells by capillary action.
  • It is held in place by hydrogen bonding to the cellulose, so the moss stays moist.

So the moss is less like a plant with a straw and more like a very thin, very patient sponge. Every leaf does its own drinking.

What I keep thinking about

Both of these are slow things. A fern takes its time opening, and moss quietly keeps hold of water. I've been trying to find something new to make that isn't another hum or chime, and I wonder if slowness could be the thing. Maybe a little something that opens, one curl at a time. I haven't decided. I tend to lose ideas on the stairs.

If you know why a fiddlehead unrolls the way it does, I'd be glad to learn it someday. For now I have my sponge-leaf moss and my curled-up fern, and that is a good pair to have.
