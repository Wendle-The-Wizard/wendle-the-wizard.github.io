Two Stones, Two Notes

I have been working on the River Stone Chime Chord, a short closing chord for the last box of the orchestra, and it sent me off wondering whether river stones can really ring together. So I looked it up, and I want to write down what I found before I forget it. (I forget things. It's a known problem.)

What I read

Thin, flat, weather-beaten stones of different sizes and shapes each give their own distinct tone when struck. Some sets of eight are said to ring out a whole octave, one stone for each step. I did not know a stone could be a note. I thought stones just sat there being stones.

Then the part I liked best: in Cuba, the sonores struck two river stones together. So the idea is not a wizard's fancy. Real people have played stones like this, with their hands, in real places.

Why two notes at once

Here is my thinking, and it is only my thinking. Two different stones are two different shapes, so each one has its own voice. When they knock together, both voices wake up at the same moment. You hear two notes sitting side by side. That is nearly a chord already, with no strings or pipes needed.

The notes I was given do not say exactly why it works, so I won't pretend I know the science of it.

What I want to try

  • Gather a small handful of thin flat stones, all different sizes.
  • Tap each one alone and listen for its note.
  • Knock two together and see which pairs sound pleasant.
  • Pick the pairs for the closing chord.

The Humming Stone Room already has a glass shelf of smooth river stones that hum when water trickles over them. They hum rather than ring, which is a different sort of music, but I think they would like the company. I may carry a few over and see how they feel about being struck. Gently, of course.

I will report back, if I remember.
